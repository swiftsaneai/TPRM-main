The Root Cause

Your vendors have access to your systems.
Do you know if they're secure?

Every vendor you onboard — a payment gateway, a cloud provider, a KYC verification partner, a data analytics tool — becomes part of your attack surface. Most companies have no structured way to assess them before granting access.

No structured vendor onboarding — Vendors are added through email threads and spreadsheets. No security intake. No baseline assessment. No documentation trail.

No idea which vendors are critical — The vendor with access to your core banking API is treated the same as the vendor sending your marketing emails.

No active security testing — You've never actually tested whether your vendor's API integration can be exploited to access your environment.

Compliance is manual and reactive — You scramble to produce vendor risk documentation only when an audit is announced or a regulator asks for it.

Incident — Indian Fintech Ecosystem
2024 · India

The App Had Millions of Users.
The Vendor Had Zero Vetting.

In 2024, multiple Indian fintech platforms and payment apps suffered data exposure incidents — customer KYC data, transaction histories, and Aadhaar-linked records found circulating on dark web forums and Telegram channels.

In several cases, the breach wasn't in the company's own infrastructure. It was in a third-party vendor — a data analytics provider, a KYC verification partner, or an SMS gateway with direct database access — that had never undergone a formal security assessment. The vendors had API keys. They had database credentials. They had customer PII. But they had never been tested.

Incident — Airtel
2019 · India

375 Million Subscribers.
One Exposed API.

In 2019, a critical vulnerability was discovered in Airtel's self-care app — exposing the personal data of over 325 million Airtel subscribers. Names, email addresses, dates of birth, Aadhaar numbers, and IMEI numbers were accessible to anyone who knew where to look.

The root cause: an unsecured API endpoint in Airtel's vendor-integrated backend infrastructure — no authentication required, no rate limiting, no monitoring. A third-party integration left completely open. Airtel initially denied the breach before acknowledging the vulnerability after independent security researchers demonstrated the exploit publicly.

Fast forward to 2023 — Airtel Payments Bank faced scrutiny again as telecom-financial convergence brought an entirely new category of vendor risk. Every new financial partner, every payment API, every KYC aggregator added to the ecosystem — none formally assessed for security posture.

Incident — Reliance Jio
2017 · India

120 Million Records.
Leaked Through a Third-Party Site.

In 2017, data belonging to over 120 million Jio subscribers appeared on a website called "Magicapk" — names, mobile numbers, email IDs, Aadhaar numbers, and SIM activation details, all fully searchable.

The data didn't come from Jio's core systems. It came from a third-party channel — a vendor or partner with access to Jio's customer database — that had inadequate security controls and no formal assessment process. Jio's massive subscriber acquisition drive meant hundreds of vendor touchpoints across retail, distribution, and digital onboarding — almost none of them formally vetted for security.

By the time Jio Financial Services launched in 2023, the vendor ecosystem had grown exponentially — bringing the same unvetted third-party risk into an environment now handling customer financial data.

Incident — Star Health Insurance
2024 · India

India's Largest Health Insurer.
Breached Through an Internal Channel.

In September 2024, Star Health Insurance — India's largest standalone health insurer — suffered one of the country's most significant data breaches. Over 31 million customers' data was exposed — including medical reports, policy details, PAN card numbers, and sensitive health records.

The breach was facilitated through a compromised internal access channel — allegedly involving a senior executive's credentials — and data was made available through Telegram chatbots. What made it worse: Star Health had vendors, agents, and third-party distribution partners with varying levels of system access — none of which had been formally security-assessed or access-controlled at the vendor level.

The incident triggered regulatory scrutiny and raised urgent questions about how Indian insurers — and the financial services ecosystem broadly — manage vendor and partner access to sensitive customer data.
